# Introduction

Lung cancer is a group of cancers that begins in the lungs. It is one of the most common cancers in the United States and is also the leading cause of cancer-related death.

There are two major categories of lung cancer:

- **Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)**
- **Small cell lung cancer (SCLC)**

Understanding how frequently lung cancer occurs, how it is detected, what increases a person's risk, how it is staged, and what treatments are available can help explain why early detection is so important.

---

## <span id="frequency">How frequently does lung cancer occur?</span>

Lung cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed cancers in the United States.

According to the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, approximately **229,410 new cases of lung and bronchus cancer are estimated for 2026** in the United States.

Approximately **124,990 deaths** are also estimated for 2026.

This represents approximately:

- **10.8% of all new cancer cases**
- **20.0% of all cancer deaths**

Lung cancer occurs primarily among older adults. Most people diagnosed with lung cancer are age 65 or older.

### Why is this significant?

Lung cancer is particularly serious because it can grow and spread before a person realizes that anything is wrong.

## <span id="detection">How is lung cancer detected?</span>

Lung cancer can be discovered in several different ways.

Sometimes a person develops symptoms that cause a doctor to investigate further. In other cases, lung cancer is discovered during screening or while doctors are investigating another medical condition.

### Common symptoms

Possible symptoms include:

- Persistent coughing
- Coughing up blood
- Chest pain
- Shortness of breath
- Wheezing
- Hoarseness
- Unexplained weight loss
- Fatigue
- Repeated respiratory infections

Having these symptoms does **not** automatically mean someone has lung cancer. Many other conditions can cause similar symptoms.

### Low-dose CT screening

For people at sufficiently high risk, doctors may recommend **low-dose computed tomography (LDCT)** screening.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force currently recommends annual LDCT screening for adults who:

- Are between **50 and 80 years old**
- Have at least a **20 pack-year smoking history**
- Currently smoke or quit within the previous 15 years

A pack-year is calculated using the number of packs smoked per day multiplied by the number of years a person smoked.
